Stale Model Alarm

A bank's AI loan model learns from old loans, and the world changes. This page replays real SBA loans from 2004–2010 to show whether a model could have noticed it was going out of date.

This project is a prototype and is not financial advice. It never approves or rejects a loan; it tells a person when to look closer.

How to read this page (30 seconds)

  1. Pick a year below. "FY2006" is the government's fiscal year: loans approved from October 2005 to September 2006.
  2. Read the colored box. It says whether the loan model needed a check-up that year.
  3. Compare two numbers: what the model predicted would default vs. what actually defaulted. A big gap means the model is out of date.

The model learned from loans approved in 2001–2002. Everything shown is a replay of a finished analysis of real loans, not a live system. Hover over any dotted term for a plain-English meaning.

      How it works

      1. AI model 1, default risk. A gradient-boosting classifier learns from 81,624 loans approved in FY2001–2002 which application details go with default. FY2003 is used only to calibrate its probabilities and pick its flagging threshold.
      2. AI model 2, drift detector. For each later year, a second classifier is shown applications from the training years and from that year, without any repayment outcomes, and tries to tell them apart. Its AUC is the drift score.
      3. Baselines. The risk model is compared with predicting the average rate for everyone and with an industry lookup table. The drift alarm is compared with the industry-standard PSI check and with a label-based monitor that watches early defaults.
      4. Honest test. Each year is judged only after the fact, against how its loans actually turned out.
      Methodology, limitations and what this does not show
      • No lead time demonstrated. The drift alarm first fired for FY2005, the same year as the simple label monitor. It needs no outcome data and explains what changed, but in this data it was not earlier.
      • Drift is not failure. The drift score keeps rising in FY2009–10 while the (incomplete) default rate falls. An alarm means "review the model", not "defaults are coming".
      • Policy changes, not just economics. The biggest drivers include SBA guarantee share, the revolving-credit coding and the end of the LowDoc program.
      • Outcome-selected data. The file only contains loans whose outcome was known by 2014, so recent default rates are overstated. FY2008–10 are marked outcome-incomplete.
      • Leak removed. Loan term (Term) behaves like it was rewritten after loans went bad. With it, AUC is about 0.96; it is excluded.
      • Generous to the model. The model was trained with outcomes of 2001–03 loans that a real bank would not have known until years later. It still went badly wrong.
      • One crisis. This is a case study of a single downturn, not a general law. No protected-group fairness audit is possible: the data has no demographic fields.

      Plain-English glossary

      Fiscal year (FY)
      The government's year, October to September. FY2006 = Oct 2005 to Sep 2006.
      Default / charged off
      The bank gave up collecting and wrote the loan off as a loss.
      Model predicted
      The average chance of default the loan model gave this year's loans.
      Actual ÷ predicted
      1.0× means the model expected the right number of defaults. 2.0× means twice as many defaulted as it expected.
      Drift score
      A second AI tries to tell this year's applications from the training years' (without ever seeing which loans defaulted). 0.5 = it can't tell, so applicants look the same. Near 1.0 = very different. The alarm goes on above the line.
      AUC
      How well the model ranks risky loans above safe ones. 0.5 = a coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.
      Brier score
      How far the predicted percentages were from what happened. Lower is better.
      PSI
      The banking industry's standard check for how much one application detail (like loan size) has shifted. Above about 0.25 is usually called large.
      Label monitor
      A simpler alarm: count how many loans approved two years ago have already defaulted.
      Mature / too early
      "Mature" years have at least 7 years of follow-up, so we can judge them. "Too early" years still had many unfinished loans when the data ends.
