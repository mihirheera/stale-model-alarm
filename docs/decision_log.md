# Decision log

Every research choice that could change a result, why it was made, and the evidence. Numbers come from
`results/audit.json` and `results/cohorts.csv` (produced by `python -m sma.audit`).

| ID | Decision | Evidence | Effect on the research question |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **Remove `Term` (loan length) and anything built from it, including the paper's "RealEstate" flag (Term ≥ 240).** | In fully mature cohorts (FY1990–2000), loans with a round term (a multiple of 12 months) defaulted **0.8%** of the time; loans with an "odd" term defaulted **29.6%**. Within the same range (61–84 months) it is 0.5% vs 23.8%; exactly 84 months 0.3% vs 83 months 10.3%. A contract length set on approval day cannot plausibly create a 30-fold difference. The odd term does *not* equal how long the loan ran before charge-off (correlation 0.05), so I do not know the mechanism. My working hypothesis is that the field was rewritten after loans ran into trouble (for example on restructuring). | Term may carry outcome information, so it is excluded as a precaution. This is a suspected recording problem, not proven leakage. A model with Term reaches AUC 0.95–0.97 on FY2004–07 (`results.json`, E7); I report that as a warning sign, not a result. |
| D2 | Ignore cohorts before FY1990. | FY1985–88 have about 2,000 loans a year and 40–49% default rates, versus 2–7% for FY1990–95. This suggests old records were kept selectively. | None: they are outside every period I use. |
| D3 | **Main evaluation = FY2004–2007. FY2008–2010 are shown but labelled "outcome-incomplete".** | The file only contains loans whose outcome was known by 2014 (Li et al. 2018, footnote 1 and section 3.3). Only 68% of charge-offs happen within 7 years of disbursement (FY1995–2000 cohorts). Coverage vs. official SBA approval counts: FY2007 file has 71,649 of 99,603 approved 7(a) loans (72%); FY2008 39,458 / 69,441 (57%); FY2009 19,103 / 41,273 (46%); FY2010 16,828 / 46,922 (36%). Source: SBA "Table 3 – Number of Approved Loans by Program" (archived at webharvest.gov), which includes later-cancelled loans. | The file's default rate for late cohorts is **too high** because unfinished loans (mostly future repayments) are missing. Conservative check: if every missing FY2007 loan had been repaid, the FY2007 default rate would be 30,660 / 99,603 = **30.8%**, still far above the ~12% of FY2001–2002. The crisis jump is real; its exact size is uncertain. |
| D4 | **Model-building period = FY2001–2003 (was FY1998–2003): train FY2001–02, calibrate FY2003; drift reference = all three.** | NAICS industry is missing for 57% of FY1998 loans, 41% (1999), 44% (2000), 0.2% (2001), 0% after. The urban/rural flag is "undefined" for 99% (1998), 62% (1999), 1% (2000+). | Avoids the drift detector "discovering" that the SBA started recording industry codes. Still 81,624 training loans and 58,000 validation loans. |
| D5 | FY2003 is used only to choose the decision threshold and to calibrate probabilities. | Calibration must only use information available before the test years. | Test years 2004+ never influence any model setting. |
| D6 | Fix two-digit-year dates (e.g. '68' was read as 2068). | Max approval date was 2068 before the fix. | Affects dates only (approval/disbursement/charge-off), not fiscal year. |
| D7 | Codes other than Y/N in `RevLineCr` and `LowDoc` → "unknown", not guessed. | Li et al. call them data-entry errors. | Their changing share over time is a *recording* change; robustness check E7 reruns drift without these fields. |
| D8 | The `LowDoc` program shows 0% use from FY2006. | `results/cohorts.csv`. | A genuine policy change, not an economic one. Reported separately so it is not mistaken for borrower risk. |

Later documentation audit (2026-11-15, no change to code logic or results): wording corrected so that Term is described as suspected contamination, dollar figures as gross charge-offs, and the leave-one-out "1 false alarm in 3" as a property of the threshold rule rather than a measured rate. A clean clone reproduced `results.json` exactly.

Open uncertainty: the exact mechanism behind D1 is unknown; D3's coverage numbers use official approvals, which include loans later cancelled, so the 30.8% bound is conservative (the true rate is likely higher).
